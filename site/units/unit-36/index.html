Postwar Poetry and Abstraction

Expression after modernism

Unit plan

Purpose. The course concludes by asking what poetry and painting can still make present after narrative certainty, traditional symbolism, and stable representation have been reduced or abandoned.

Visual plan. A deliberately spare four-work progression from geometric reduction through gesture, color field, and quiet postwar grid.

Looking at the Images

Mondrian reduces painting to vertical and horizontal relations, primary color, and unequal balance. Pollock makes the record of bodily movement spread across the whole field. Rothko slows the encounter into hovering color and uncertain depth, while Martin’s grid replaces dramatic gesture with minute repetition and attention.

The sequence should be viewed slowly. The relevant question is not merely what the paintings “represent,” but what kinds of sustained looking their different degrees of reduction make possible.

“Of the genesis of her paintings, Martin said, “When I first made a grid I happened to be thinking of the innocence of trees and then this grid came into my mind and I thought it represented innocence, and I still do, and so I painted it and then I was satisfied. I thought, this is my vision.” Martin rendered fine vertical lines and lightly shaded horizontal bands in oil and pencil, softening the geometric grid, which in this case seems to expand beyond the confines of the canvas. For Martin the grid evoked not a human measure but an ethereal onethe boundless order or transcendent reality associated with Eastern philosophies.”

Abstract Expressionism developed in New York during and after the Second World War. Although Pollock’s layered gestures and Rothko’s hovering fields of colour look radically different, both use large-scale abstraction to create an immediate physical experience.

Featured Poems and Texts

The Plain Sense of Things

After autumn has stripped away the leaves, Stevens confronts a landscape that appears exhausted of imaginative richness. The great house has diminished, the greenhouse needs paint, and the pond seems reduced to mud, dirty water, and silence. Yet the poem’s apparent rejection of imagination reverses upon itself: even such barrenness must be perceived, shaped, and imagined.

The poem therefore does not arrive at a world completely free of imagination. Instead, it asks whether “plain reality” can ever be encountered without the mind’s activity—and whether absence, failure, and emptiness are themselves imaginative constructions.

The Plain Sense of Things

Wallace Stevens

First published in The Nation, 6 December 1952; collected in The Collected Poems of Wallace Stevens (1954).

After the leaves have fallen, we return
To a plain sense of things. It is as if
We had come to an end of the imagination,
Inanimate in an inert savoir.

It is difficult even to choose the adjective
For this blank cold, this sadness without cause.
The great structure has become a minor house.
No turban walks across the lessened floors.

The greenhouse never so badly needed paint.
The chimney is fifty years old and slants to one side.
A fantastic effort has failed, a repetition
In a repetitiousness of men and flies.

Yet the absence of the imagination had
Itself to be imagined. The great pond,
The plain sense of it, without reflections, leaves,
Mud, water like dirty glass, expressing silence

Of a sort, silence of a rat come out to see,
The great pond and its waste of the lilies, all this
Had to be imagined as an inevitable knowledge,
Required, as a necessity requires.

Of Mere Being

Stevens imagines a point beyond “the last thought,” where the mind encounters something that cannot be reduced to human reason, emotion, or interpretation. The palm and its brilliant bird belong at once to an imagined landscape and to a reality seemingly independent of the person imagining it.

The bird’s foreign song does not explain existence or supply a human message. It simply sounds, while its feathers shine. The poem presents “mere being” not as emptiness, but as a strange, radiant presence at the edge of thought.

Of Mere Being

Wallace Stevens

Published posthumously; collected in The Palm at the End of the Mind: Selected Poems and a Play.

The palm at the end of the mind,
Beyond the last thought, rises
In the bronze decor,

A gold-feathered bird
Sings in the palm, without human meaning,
Without human feeling, a foreign song.

You know then that it is not the reason
That makes us happy or unhappy.
The bird sings. Its feathers shine.

The palm stands on the edge of space.
The wind moves slowly in the branches.
The bird’s fire-fangled feathers dangle down.
