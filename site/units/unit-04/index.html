Unit 04

Lyric and Desire

Sappho

If Homer and Virgil presented the heroic, the epic, the historical, the mythological … and Keats and Rilke contemplated enigmatic objects of classical art and described their effects on the observer, Sappho gives us the intensely personal, the inner experience, the seemingly private — almost as if overheard.

Fragment 31

Sappho, trans. Anne Carson

He seems to me equal to gods that man
whoever he is who opposite you
sits and listens close
   to your sweet speaking

and lovely laughing—oh it
puts the heart in my chest on wings
for when I look at you, even a moment, no speaking
   is left in me

no: tongue breaks and thin
fire is racing under skin
and in eyes no sight and drumming
   fills ears

and cold sweat holds me and shaking
grips me all, greener than grass
I am and dead—or almost
   I seem to me.

But all is to be dared, because even a person of poverty

Essential Questions

  • Recall that Rilke seemed to indicate that the sculpture of Apollo surviving as ‘merely’ a fragment only intensifies its force. What of Sappho’s work itself surviving only in fragments?
  • Why the strange grammar? “I am and dead.” “No: breaks and thin” leaves off.
  • What could “greener than grass” mean?
  • The fragment cuts off abruptly, just as seemingly a new line of thought began. Where do you think Sappho went next?
Advanced Questions Show Hide
  • Why say “I seem to me” rather than “I am”?

Vocabulary

  • Lyric — poetry of concentrated voice, feeling, perception, or inward experience. Lyric usually does not tell a long public story like epic; it gives shape to a speaker’s immediate state of mind or body.

  • Eros — desire, especially erotic or passionate longing. In Greek poetry, eros is not merely “romance.”

Additional Poems and Texts

Poem 51

Gaius Valerius Catullus

Translated from the Latin by Charles Martin, from The Poems of Catullus (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1989).

To me that man seems like a god in heaven,
seems—may I say it?—greater than all gods are,
who sits by you & without interruption
 watches you, listens

to your light laughter, which casts such confusion
onto my senses, Lesbia, that when I
gaze at you merely, all of my well-chosen
 words are forgotten

as my tongue thickens & a subtle fire
runs through my body while my ears deafened
by their own ringing & at once my eyes are
 covered in darkness!

Leisure, Catullus. More than just a nuisance,
leisure: you riot, overmuch enthusing.
Fabulous cities & their sometime kings have
 died of such leisure.

For three stanzas Catullus closely follows Sappho. But then a dramatic departure. Martin translates the repeated Latin otium as “leisure,” but the Latin word carries a larger set of associations. It can mean freedom from public business, unoccupied time, retirement, idleness, or a life withdrawn from civic duties.

Teacher notes Show Hide

  • Emphasis the lyrical focus on the intense inner experience.

  • If Homer and Virgil intended a city, a civilization as audience, here the audience feels to be somewhere between small and intimate to non-existent.

  • Fragment 31 is composed in, well, Sapphic Stanzas. The first thing to notice about these is:

      		Long line
  		Long line 
  		Lone line
  		Short line

With the short fourth line uses to make some sort of turn, or compression, or pack some sort of punch. The three longer lines tend to build towards something, then the compressed fourth line wallops you with something, or collapses, reverses, deflates, etc.

  • Notice that Sappho does not simply instruct the (overhearing) audience that a feeling is intense or overwhelming. She careful describes, directly, an experience.
