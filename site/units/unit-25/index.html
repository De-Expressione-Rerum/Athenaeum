Unit 25

Why Paint at All?

Photography and the crisis of representation

Unit plan

Purpose. Photography changes the meaning of likeness, evidence, memory, and artistic labor. Painting must now make a new claim for what it can do.

Visual plan. A four-image sequence from the earliest surviving photograph to photography’s increasing command of detail and time, concluding with Courbet’s large allegorical claim for painting.

Looking at the Images

Niépce’s image is difficult to read because the long exposure distributes light across different sides of the buildings. Daguerre’s boulevard appears nearly empty, not because Paris lacked crowds, but because moving figures did not remain still long enough to register. Talbot’s open door demonstrates photography’s appetite for texture, framing, and ordinary objects.

Courbet’s Painter’s Studio then makes an enormous, self-conscious claim for painting after photography: the artist gathers society, landscape, models, patrons, and allegory into a scene no camera could simply encounter.

Featured Poems and Texts

This Is a Photograph of Me

Atwood’s poem begins as the description of an old and indistinct photograph. The speaker directs our eyes across its blurred surface, gradually identifying a tree, a house, a lake, and distant hills. The poem therefore teaches us how to look before it tells us what we are looking for.

Its parenthetical revelation transforms the photograph completely. What appeared to be an empty landscape contains a human presence hidden beneath the water. The poem asks whether sustained attention can recover what an image obscures—and whether the dead, the forgotten, or the overlooked can make themselves visible through language.

This Is a Photograph of Me

Margaret Atwood

From The Circle Game (1964; revised edition, 1966). Text supplied for inclusion by the course author.

It was taken some time ago.
At first it seems to be
a smeared
print: blurred lines and grey flecks
blended with the paper;

then, as you scan
it, you see in the left-hand corner
a thing that is like a branch: part of a tree
(balsam or spruce) emerging
and, to the right, halfway up
what ought to be a gentle
slope, a small frame house.

In the background there is a lake,
and beyond that, some low hills.

(The photograph was taken
the day after I drowned.

I am in the lake, in the center
of the picture, just under the surface.

It is difficult to say where
precisely, or to say
how large or small I am:
the effect of water
on light is a distortion

but if you look long enough,
eventually
you will be able to see me.)
