Ode on a Grecian Urn (1819)

John Keats

Thou still unravish’d bride of quietness,
  Thou foster-child of silence and slow time,
Sylvan historian, who canst thus express
  A flowery tale more sweetly than our rhyme:
What leaf-fring’d legend haunts about thy shape
  Of deities or mortals, or of both,
   In Tempe or the dales of Arcady?
  What men or gods are these? What maidens loth?
What mad pursuit? What struggle to escape?
   What pipes and timbrels? What wild ecstasy?

Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard
  Are sweeter; therefore, ye soft pipes, play on;
Not to the sensual ear, but, more endear’d,
  Pipe to the spirit ditties of no tone:
Fair youth, beneath the trees, thou canst not leave
  Thy song, nor ever can those trees be bare;
   Bold Lover, never, never canst thou kiss,
Though winning near the goal yet, do not grieve;
  She cannot fade, though thou hast not thy bliss,
   For ever wilt thou love, and she be fair!

Ah, happy, happy boughs! that cannot shed
  Your leaves, nor ever bid the Spring adieu;
And, happy melodist, unwearied,
  For ever piping songs for ever new;
More happy love! more happy, happy love!
  For ever warm and still to be enjoy’d,
   For ever panting, and for ever young;
All breathing human passion far above,
  That leaves a heart high-sorrowful and cloy’d,
   A burning forehead, and a parching tongue.

Who are these coming to the sacrifice?
  To what green altar, O mysterious priest,
Lead’st thou that heifer lowing at the skies,
  And all her silken flanks with garlands drest?
What little town by river or sea shore,
  Or mountain-built with peaceful citadel,
   Is emptied of this folk, this pious morn?
And, little town, thy streets for evermore
  Will silent be; and not a soul to tell
   Why thou art desolate, can e’er return.

O Attic shape! Fair attitude! with brede
  Of marble men and maidens overwrought,
With forest branches and the trodden weed;
  Thou, silent form, dost tease us out of thought
As doth eternity: Cold Pastoral!
  When old age shall this generation waste,
   Thou shalt remain, in midst of other woe
Than ours, a friend to man, to whom thou say’st,
  “Beauty is truth, truth beauty,—that is all
   Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.”

Archaic Torso of Apollo (1908)

Rainer Maria Rilke

We cannot know his legendary head
with eyes like ripening fruit. And yet his torso
is still suffused with brilliance from inside,
like a lamp, in which his gaze, now turned to low,

gleams in all its power. Otherwise
the curved breast could not dazzle you so, nor could
a smile run through the placid hips and thighs
to that dark center where procreation flared.

Otherwise this stone would seem defaced
beneath the translucent cascade of the shoulders
and would not glisten like a wild beast’s fur:

would not, from all the borders of itself,
burst like a star: for here there is no place
that does not see you. You must change your life.

Essential Questions

  • Who or what is Keats addressing? Notice “Thou”
  • Why slow time?
  • “A flowery tale more sweetly than our rhyme”; Can images speak more than words? What about the paradox that this is itself written in a poem?
  • Who or what is Rilke addressing?
  • What could it possibly mean to say ‘Beauty is Truth, Truth Beauty’?
  • What does Rilke say makes this fragment or ruin still powerful?
  • What is the Volta in Rilke’s Archaic Torso of Apollo?
  • How is it that contemplating a fragment of ancient art could compel such a lightning bolt?
Advanced Questions Show Hide
  • How do these examples of ekphrasis differ from the ancient examples of the previous units?
  • Is there some way in which a fragment or a partial ruin could be more powerful than the whole?
  • Is the urn — a work of art — greater or lesser than lived, mortal life in its permanence, stillness?

Vocabulary

  • Lyric poem — a short or concentrated poem focused on a speaker’s feeling, thought, or experience
  • Ode — a lyric poem that addresses, praises, reflects on, or meditates upon a person, object, idea, place, or experience
  • Sonnet — a 14-line lyric poem that develops one central idea or feeling and usually includes a volta, or turn in thought
  • Volta — the “turn” in a sonnet, where the poem shifts in thought, feeling, argument, or address
  • Epiphany — a moment of revelation
  • Imperative — a command form: “You must…”
  • Apostrophe — directly addressing someone or something absent, dead, or unable to answer.
Teacher notes Show Hide
  • Notice the double meaning of “still”: “Thou still unravish’d bride of quietness”
  • Notice that both works are full of mystery. Keats asks many unanswered questions of the urn’s images, and it is essential that Rilke describes a partial ruin.
  • “Maidens loth” suggests reluctance. It is not all Arkady pastoral idyll. The erotic, and even violence, looms.
  • Notice the repetition: “Bold Lover, never, never canst thou kiss”. Keats first suggests that there may be something beautiful in trees which can never be bare, but then what of the lover whose pursuit will “never, never” attain his beloved?
  • In stanza 4, Keats no imagines what cannot be seen, the town left behind by the pious procession; a town that will forever be empty.
  • What does “as doth eternity” mean?
  • “Cold Pastoral!”
  • And of course: what is the final couplet doing? Beauty is truth? Truth, beauty? That’s all ye need to know? Who is saying this? Keats? The urn? Does anybody mean it?
  • Notice the use of form. Keats’ stanzas start with a Shakespearean quatrain, with a simple rhyming scheme. But then these are following by less formulaic and more varied sestets; almost as if the speaker starts out sure of something, but becomes less so.
  • Keats contemplates the stillness of a work of art, Rilke is stunned by how alive, forceful, even fecund a headless old torso can be. Apollo’s torso even radiates beyond its borders, and shimmers like the skin of a wild beast or predator.
  • Apollo moves from being blind, to being all-seeing.
  • Compare the erotic in each.
  • Compare absence in each.
  • Remember that Rilke is originally German. Printed above is perhaps the most accessible. But it may lack some of the original German’s strangeness and compactness. See the filling 1957 translation by C.F. MacIntyre:

Never will we know his fabulous head
where the eyes’ apples slowly ripened. Yet
his torso glows: a candelabrum set
before his gaze which is pushed back and hid,
restrained and shining. Else the curving breast
could not thus blind you, nor through the soft turn
of the loins could this smile easily have passed
into the bright groins where the genitals burned.

Else stood this stone a fragment and defaced,
with lucent body from the shoulders falling,
too short, not gleaming like a lion’s fell;

nor would this star have shaken the shackles off,
bursting with light, until there is no place
that does not see you. You must change your life.
