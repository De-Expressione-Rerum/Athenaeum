Unit 33

World War I

Owen, Rosenberg, and the shattered image

Unit plan

Purpose. The mechanical force celebrated by the avant-garde encounters industrialized war. Heroic rhetoric, bodily integrity, stable perspective, and confidence in civilization all become difficult to sustain.

Visual plan. A five-image sequence moving from Expressionist psychological fracture to machinery, ruined landscape, gas warfare, and prophetic animal catastrophe.

Artworks and Images

War and the Shattered Image

1 / 1

Looking at the Images

Kirchner’s amputated hand is imagined rather than documentary, but it makes psychic damage visible before the soldier has physically lost the limb. Nevinson organizes gunners and machine into a single hard mechanism. Nash lets the ruined landscape indict the optimistic title, while Dix reduces the attacking soldiers to masked, advancing instruments.

Marc’s doomed animals, painted before the full reality of the war was known, can be read as a prophetic collapse of the utopian energy once attached to modern form.

Featured Poems and Texts

Dulce et Decorum Est

Wilfred Owen

Text published posthumously in Poems (1920)

Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,
Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,
Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs,
And towards our distant rest began to trudge.
Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots,
But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame, all blind;
Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots
Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.

Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!–An ecstasy of fumbling
Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time,
But someone still was yelling out and stumbling
And flound’ring like a man in fire or lime.–
Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,
As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.

In all my dreams before my helpless sight
He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.

If in some smothering dreams, you too could pace
Behind the wagon that we flung him in,
And watch the white eyes writhing in his face,
His hanging face, like a devil’s sick of sin,
If you could hear, at every jolt, the blood
Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs
Bitter as the cud
Of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues,–
My friend, you would not tell with such high zest
To children ardent for some desperate glory,
The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est
Pro patria mori.

Break of Day in the Trenches

Isaac Rosenberg

First published in Poetry (1916)

The darkness crumbles away.
It is the same old druid Time as ever,
Only a live thing leaps my hand,
A queer sardonic rat,
As I pull the parapet’s poppy
To stick behind my ear.
Droll rat, they would shoot you if they knew
Your cosmopolitan sympathies.
Now you have touched this English hand
You will do the same to a German—
Soon, no doubt, if it be your pleasure
To cross the sleeping green between.
It seems you inwardly grin as you pass
Strong eyes, fine limbs, haughty athletes,
Less chanced than you for life,
Bonds to the whims of murder,
Sprawled in the bowels of the earth,
The torn fields of France.
What do you see in our eyes
At the shrieking iron and flame
Hurled through still heavens?
What quaver—what heart aghast?
Poppies whose roots are in man’s veins
Drop, and are ever dropping;
But mine in my ear is safe—
Just a little white with the dust.
