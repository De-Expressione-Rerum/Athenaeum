Dada and Surrealism

Anti-art, chance, and dream

Unit plan

Purpose. Dada attacks the cultural authority of art after the war; Surrealism seeks another order in dream, chance, desire, and the unconscious. They belong in one unit but should remain visibly distinct.

Visual plan. A six-work survey divided explicitly into Dada and Surrealism. Duchamp’s readymade is restored to its historical Dada context.

Looking at the Images

Duchamp’s readymade shifts attention from skilled manufacture to selection, naming, institutional display, and argument. Höch and Schwitters cut the printed debris of modern life into new and unstable relations. Dada’s destruction of coherence is deliberate and polemical.

The Surrealist works pursue a different aim. Ernst, Dalí, and Magritte use recognizable objects with impossible relations, making dream, memory, and language disturb the ordinary rules of reality.

Featured Poems and Texts

To Make a Dadaist Poem

Tzara turns poetic composition into a repeatable procedure. Instead of choosing words for their meaning, sound, or place in a deliberate design, the writer takes language from a newspaper, cuts it into fragments, and allows chance to determine their order. The work attacks conventional ideas of skill, coherence, and individual inspiration—but its final joke complicates that attack: the accidental poem will still “resemble” its maker.

The procedure also links literary Dada to collage. Printed language becomes physical material to be cut, shuffled, and recombined, while the author’s role shifts from composing a finished work to devising and carrying out a process.

To Make a Dadaist Poem

Tristan Tzara

Fresh English translation from “Pour faire un poème dadaïste,” first published in Littérature, no. 15 (July–August 1920), p. 18. The original page is signed “Aa l’Antiphilosophe et Tristan Tzara.” U.S. public-domain source text.

Take a newspaper.

Take a pair of scissors.

Choose an article from the newspaper that is as long as you want your poem to be.

Cut out the article.

Then carefully cut out each of the words that make up the article and put them into a bag.

Shake gently.

Then take out each cutting one after another, in the order in which they leave the bag.

Copy conscientiously.

The poem will resemble you.

And there you are: an infinitely original writer, with a charming sensibility—even if the vulgar fail to understand it.

The Earth Is Blue Like an Orange

Éluard begins with an apparent impossibility and immediately denies that it is an error: “words do not lie.” The poem asks the reader to accept poetic images not as failed logical statements but as new relations created by desire, resemblance, sound, and imagination.

The beloved gradually becomes inseparable from the visible world. Her mouth, nakedness, beauty, dawn, windows, leaves, and sunlight flow into one another without explanatory transitions. Unlike Dada’s deliberate use of accident and disruption, Éluard’s Surrealism discovers an alternative coherence in images that rational description would keep apart.

The Earth Is Blue Like an Orange

Paul Éluard

Fresh English translation from the untitled poem beginning “La terre est bleue comme une orange,” in L’Amour la poésie (1929), part I, “Premièrement.” Public-domain source text.

The earth is blue like an orange
Never a mistake words do not lie
They no longer give you anything to sing
Now it is the kisses’ turn to understand one another
The mad and the loves
She her mouth of union
All the secrets all the smiles
And what garments of indulgence
To believe her wholly naked.

The wasps bloom green
Dawn puts around its neck
A necklace of windows
Wings cover the leaves
You have all the joys of sunlight
All the sun upon the earth
Along the paths of your beauty.
