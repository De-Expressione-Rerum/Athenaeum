The Shield of Achilles

Homer, Iliad 18.478–608

First he fashioned the shield, immense and strong,
richly adorned, and round its outer edge
he cast a triple rim, bright as the sun,
and from it hung a silver-bearing strap.
Fivefold the shield itself; and over it
the god, with all his wisdom, wrought a world.

There he made earth, and heaven, and sea,
the tireless sun, the moon at fullest light,
and all the signs that crown the face of heaven:
Pleiades and Hyades, Orion’s strength,
and the Bear, whom men also call the Wain,
who turns in place and watches great Orion,
alone unbathed by Ocean’s flowing stream.

Upon it too he made two cities fair,
filled with the speech and labor of mankind.
In one were marriages and marriage feasts:
by torchlight from their chambers brides were led
through streets alive with song; the wedding hymn
rose high, and young men whirled in dancing rings,
while flutes and lyres called clear beneath their hands.
Women stood wondering in the shadowed doors.

And in the marketplace a quarrel rose.
Two men contended over blood-price owed
for one slain dead. One cried that all was paid;
the other swore that he had taken nothing.
Both called for judgment, eager for an end.
The people shouted, favoring each in turn,
while heralds held them back. The elders sat
on polished stones within a sacred ring,
and in their hands they held the heralds’ staves.
Then each in turn rose up and gave his doom.
Between them lay two talents of pure gold,
a prize for him whose judgment proved most just.

Around the other city lay two hosts,
their armor glittering, bright in siege array.
Two counsels pleased them: either utterly
to sack the town, or share among themselves
all treasure that the lovely city held.
But those within would not submit or yield.
They armed for ambush. On the walls there stood
their wives, their little children, and the old,
keeping the city. Out the warriors went,
and Ares led them, with Athena’s might,
both wrought in gold, and golden were their robes,
tall, beautiful, and splendid in their arms,
as gods are; smaller were the men around.

They came where they had chosen ambuscade,
beside a river, watering-place of herds,
and there they hid themselves in shining bronze.
Apart from them two watchmen took their stand,
to spy the sheep and shambling cattle’s tread.
Soon came the flocks and cattle, and with them
two herdsmen piping, innocent of doom.
Then from their hiding-place the armed men sprang;
they seized the herds, cut off the white-fleeced sheep,
and struck the herdsmen down beside their beasts.

The besiegers, seated in their place of speech,
heard sudden tumult rising from the herds.
At once they mounted high-stepping horses,
pursued, and overtook them by the stream.
There, face to face, they fought with bronze-tipped spears.
And Strife was there, and Tumult, and dark Fate,
who held one newly wounded, one unhurt,
and dragged one dead man by the feet through dust.
Upon her shoulders hung a garment red
with human blood. They struggled, living-like,
and each side drew away the bodies of the slain.

Then he made a field, soft, rich, and broad,
three times ploughed over. Many ploughmen there
drove teams across it, turning to and fro.
And when a man had reached the field’s far edge
and wheeled his team, a servant came to him
and put into his hands a cup of wine,
honey-sweet; then again he took the furrow,
eager to reach the deep field’s farther side.
Behind them, though the soil was wrought of gold,
the land grew dark, as newly broken earth:
a marvel fashioned by the god’s own hand.

And there he made a king’s estate in harvest.
Reapers moved through the grain with sharpened hooks;
thick fell the handfuls down along the swath,
and binders gathered them in bands of straw.
Three binders stood nearby; behind them boys
collected armfuls of the fallen grain
and bore them on. The king, in silence, stood
beside the swath, staff in his hand, rejoicing.
Beneath an oak the heralds set a feast:
they dressed a great ox newly sacrificed,
while women scattered barley, white and bright,
to make the harvest-supper for the men.

He made a vineyard, heavy-clustered, fair,
golden and beautiful. The grapes hung dark;
the vines were propped on silver stakes. Around
he drew a ditch of blue, and round that ditch
a fence of tin. One pathway led within,
where gatherers passed in season of the vintage.
Girls and young men, light-hearted, carried fruit
in woven baskets. In their midst a boy
played sweetly on a clear-toned lyre and sang
a delicate song; the others followed him
with beating feet, with cries, and dancing joy.

He made a herd of cattle, straight of horn.
The cows were wrought of gold and tin; they lowed
as from the farmyard toward the pastureland
they hurried, by a murmuring river’s side
and waving reeds. Four golden herdsmen went
beside them, and nine quick-foot dogs behind.
But from the foremost herd two dreadful lions
had seized a bellowing bull and dragged him off.
Young men and dogs came running after them.
The lions tore the great hide, drank the blood,
and ate the entrails, while the herdsmen urged
their dogs in vain. The hounds shrank back from teeth;
they stood nearby and barked, but dared not bite.

Then the renowned lame god made pastureland,
a valley beautiful and wide, with sheep,
white flocks, and huts, and roofed folds, and pens.

And there he made a dancing-place, like one
which Daedalus in spacious Knossos wrought
for Ariadne of the lovely hair.
There boys and maidens, worth rich bridal gifts,
danced hand in hand, each holding by the wrist.
The maidens wore fine linen; the young men
wore tunics woven well and faintly gleaming,
soft with oil. The girls had lovely wreaths;
the men had golden knives from silver belts.
Now all together, with accomplished feet,
they ran in circles, as a potter tries
his wheel between his hands to see it run;
now back and forth they moved in facing lines.
A crowd stood round that longed-for dance, delighted.
Among them two skilled tumblers led the song
and whirled between the dancers as they sang.

And last, around the outer rim, he set
the mighty river Ocean, deep and strong.

Essential Questions

  • Why does Homer go to such lengths to describe Hephaestus’ crafting of this shield?
  • What does it mean that Hephaestus “wrought a world”?
  • What does the shield, which Achilles is to carry into battle, depict? What sort of world(s) is depicted? What does this say about the world outside of war?
  • Why give Achilles the world to carry with him into battle?
  • What does this suggest to us about the power and purpose of art and representation?
Advanced Questions Show Hide
  • What can we say about the tension here between the act of creation in the context of war, destruction, and violence?
  • What is the significance of the depiction of the quarrel in the marketplace?
  • Can art be redemptive?
  • Contrast with Auden’s ekphrasis (see below)
  • Describe the world depicted in Auden’s shield. What is he saying regarding warfare, modern and Bronze Age?

Vocabulary

  • Ekphrasis — a passage in which words try to make an artwork vividly present to the reader, often by describing not only what the artwork looks like, but what it seems to mean, how it moves the viewer, or what story it contains

Additional Poems and Texts

The Shield of Achilles (1952)

W. H. Auden

She looked over his shoulder
  For vines and olive trees,
Marble well-governed cities
  And ships upon untamed seas,
But there on the shining metal
  His hands had put instead
An artificial wilderness
  And a sky like lead.

A plain without a feature, bare and brown,
 No blade of grass, no sign of neighborhood,
Nothing to eat and nowhere to sit down,
 Yet, congregated on its blankness, stood
 An unintelligible multitude,
A million eyes, a million boots in line,
Without expression, waiting for a sign.

Out of the air a voice without a face
 Proved by statistics that some cause was just
In tones as dry and level as the place:
 No one was cheered and nothing was discussed;
 Column by column in a cloud of dust
They marched away enduring a belief
Whose logic brought them, somewhere else, to grief.

She looked over his shoulder
  For ritual pieties,
White flower-garlanded heifers,
  Libation and sacrifice,
But there on the shining metal
  Where the altar should have been,
She saw by his flickering forge-light
  Quite another scene.

Barbed wire enclosed an arbitrary spot
 Where bored officials lounged (one cracked a joke)
And sentries sweated for the day was hot:
 A crowd of ordinary decent folk
 Watched from without and neither moved nor spoke
As three pale figures were led forth and bound
To three posts driven upright in the ground.

The mass and majesty of this world, all
 That carries weight and always weighs the same
Lay in the hands of others; they were small
 And could not hope for help and no help came:
 What their foes liked to do was done, their shame
Was all the worst could wish; they lost their pride
And died as men before their bodies died.

She looked over his shoulder
  For athletes at their games,
Men and women in a dance
  Moving their sweet limbs
Quick, quick, to music,
  But there on the shining shield
His hands had set no dancing-floor
  But a weed-choked field.

A ragged urchin, aimless and alone,
 Loitered about that vacancy; a bird
Flew up to safety from his well-aimed stone:
 That girls are raped, that two boys knife a third,
 Were axioms to him, who’d never heard
Of any world where promises were kept,
Or one could weep because another wept.

The thin-lipped armorer,
  Hephaestos, hobbled away,
Thetis of the shining breasts
  Cried out in dismay
At what the god had wrought
  To please her son, the strong
Iron-hearted man-slaying Achilles
  Who would not live long.

Teacher notes Show Hide
  • Auden — negative or revisionary ekphrasis
  • Notes on the poetic structure of Auden’s The Shield of Achilles:
    • Notice the very deliberate use of meter, structure and form
    • We start with (implied) Thetis looking over Hephaestus’ shoulder
    • She has certain expectations of what should be depicted on such a shield: rites, ways of life, harmony, sacrifice, harvest, fertility, dancing, games, etc
      • These stanzas are in a loosely-ballad form which sounds simpler, older, song-like, ancient, resonant, folksy, natural
    • But what does she see instead? She sees a moral desert, soldiers in line, violence, execution, barbed wire, bureaucracy, emptiness
      • And notice these stanzas are in an entirely different form
      • They are longer, descriptive, and in “Rhyme Royal”:
        • with the pattern: ababbcc
        • associated with Chaucer
        • more discursive and philosophical
        • and strikes with a particularly pitiless “cc” at the end of each stanza

i.e.:
Expectation: She looked over his shoulder
  For ritual pieties,
White flower-garlanded heifers,
  Libation and sacrifice

Reality:
As three pale figures were led forth and bound
To three posts driven upright in the ground.

So for further exploration and analysis: How does Auden use the very forms of the poem to reinforce his meaning? And, never to lose track of: what is the meaning?
