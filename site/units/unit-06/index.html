Featured Poems and Texts

Catullus 101

Catullus

Translated by Robinson Ellis (1871)

Borne o’er many a land, o’er many a level of ocean,
Here to the grave I come, brother, of holy repose,
Sadly the last poor gifts, death’s simple duty, to bring thee;

Unto the silent dust vainly to murmur a cry.
Since thy form deep-shrouded an evil destiny taketh
From me, O hapless ghost, brother, O heavily ta’en,
Yet this bounty the while, these gifts ancestral of usance
Homely, the sad slight store piety grants to the tomb;
Drench’d in a brother’s tears, and weeping freshly, receive them;

Yea, take, brother, a long Ave, a timeless adieu.

Heraclitus

Callimachus

Translated by William Johnson Cory

They told me, Heraclitus, they told me you were dead,
They brought me bitter news to hear and bitter tears to shed.

I wept, as I remembered, how often you and I
Had tired the sun with talking and sent him down the sky.
And now that thou art lying, my dear old Carian guest,
A handful of grey ashes, long, long ago at rest,
Still are thy pleasant voices, thy nightingales, awake;
For Death, he taketh all away, but them he cannot take.

Vocabulary

  • Stele — a carved stone slab or pillar used as a grave marker
  • Epigram — a short, pithy poem
  • Apostrophe — direct address to someone absent or dead, as though present

Greek Funerary Epigrams

Three Greek funerary epigrams:

The Spartans at Thermopylae

On the Spartans at Thermopylae

Simonides

Greek Anthology 7.249. Translated by J. W. Mackail in Select Epigrams from the Greek Anthology (1890). Public domain.

O passer by, tell the Lacedaemonians
that we lie here obeying their orders.

Lacedaemonians means the people of Lacedaemon, another name for Sparta and its territory.

Nicoteles

On a Boy of Twelve

Callimachus

Greek Anthology 7.453. Translated by J. W. Mackail in Select Epigrams from the Greek Anthology (1890). Public domain.

Philip the father laid here
the twelve-years-old child,
his high hope, Nicoteles.

Crethis

On a Girl

Callimachus

Greek Anthology 7.459. Translated by J. W. Mackail in Select Epigrams from the Greek Anthology (1890). Public domain.

The daughters of the Samians often require
Crethis the teller of tales, who knew pretty games,
sweetest of workfellows, ever talking;
but she sleeps here the sleep to which they all must come.
