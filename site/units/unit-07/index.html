Unit 07

Courtly Life

Murasaki Shikibu and Bernart de Ventadorn

The Tale of Genji is “a classic work of Japanese literature written by the noblewoman, poet, and lady-in-waiting Murasaki Shikibu around the peak of the Heian period, in the early 11th century. It is the first novel written by a woman to have won global recognition.”

It is primarily a prose narrative, but its characters frequently compose and exchange waka — short thirty-one-syllable poems used for courtship, farewell, veiled criticism, emotional disclosure, and other socially delicate and prescribed communication.

Context of the passage: Kaoru has never ceased mourning Nakanokimi’s elder sister, Ōigimi. He speaks indirectly of his grief. Hoping perhaps to release him from this attachment, Nakanokimi reveals that a young woman resembling Ōigimi is secretly staying nearby. This is Ukifune, a previously unacknowledged half-sister.

Kaoru is intrigued, but he hesitates at the thought of transferring his feelings so suddenly from the dead woman to her living likeness.

He then addresses Nakanokimi in a waka in which he describes Ukifune as a katashiro — a likeness or substitute and onto which emotion may be transferred. Nakanokimi answers by extending and turning the metaphors in reply:

The Tale of Genji

Murasaki Shikibu

The Tale of Genji, Chapter 50, “Azumaya” (“The Eastern Cottage”)

Kaoru

If she is a likeness
of the one I once knew,
I shall keep her beside me—
an effigy on which to lay
each recurring wave of longing.

Nakanokimi

An effigy destined
to be cast into every reach
of the purification river—
who could trust such a thing
as a shadow always at one’s side?

In an instant, Nakanokimi must take up Kaoru’s waka and reply with her own, using the correct forms, elegantly constructed to adhere to the social norms of court etiquette while carefully and subtly expressing her own view and even challenging and reframing both the language and meaning of Kaoru.

The original Japanese, in this brief exchange alone, depends on a precise assemblage of courtly, ritualized vocabulary:

  • katashiro — a likeness, substitute, or ritual proxy (compare to Greek: therapōn)\
  • nademono — used in purification rites, sometimes rubbed over the body to absorb impurity\
  • misogi-gawa — the purification river\
  • seze — the shallows / rapids / reaches of the river, with further suggestive force in context

Later, Nakanokimi brings out some illustrated tales and asks Ukon to read aloud. Ukifune became too absorbed in the pictures to maintain her shyness under Nakanokimi’s gaze. In the lamplight as she looks intently before her, she appears delicately beautiful, without any discernible fault. The shape of her brow, the luminous cast of her eyes, and her gentle, aristocratic bearing recalls only one person.

“What a moving likeness,” she thought. “How can she resemble her so closely? She must take strongly after our late father. The old women used to say that my sister resembled him, while I resembled our mother. Truly, there is something overwhelming in seeing one person resemble another.”

Comparing Ukifune with Ōigimi in her mind, Nakanokimi watches her through tears.

And this is precisely the scene depicted here:

Nakanokimi, Ukon, and Ukifune occupy a palace interior divided by curtains, screens, and sliding partitions. One woman has her hair attended to, another reads, and another looks at illustrations. The elevated “blown-off roof” convention allows us to see into a space where social life depends upon restricted access, concealment, and carefully managed sight.

Troubadour

Can vei la lauzeta mover

Bernart de Ventadorn

Translated from the Occitan by A. S. Kline, From Dawn to Dawn: Troubadour Poetry (2009). Reproduced for non-commercial educational use.

When I see the lark display
His wings with joy against the day,
Forgetting, fold then fall away,
As sweetness to his heart makes way,
Such great envy then invades
My mind: I see the rest take fire,
And marvel at it, for no way
Can my heart turn from its desire.

Ah, I so dearly wished to know
Of love, yet so little learn,
For I cannot keep from loving her
Who will not have me, though I burn.
She stole my heart, and all of me,
And she herself, and worlds apart;
Lacking herself, now nothing’s left
But longing and the willing heart.

For ‘I’ has no power over ‘I’
Nor has had since the day I know
I let myself gaze in her eye,
The mirror that pleased me so.
Mirror, now I’m mirrored in you,
Profound sighs are killing me,
I lost myself as he did too
Narcissus gazing in the deep.

Of every lady I despair!
And in them I can place no trust!
Those I once would seek to cheer
Leave them cheerless now I must.
Seeing her then who won’t have me,
She who destroys me and confounds,
I doubt them all and can’t believe,
Knowing them other than they’re found.

My lady shows herself, not to my good,
A woman indeed, scorns my behest,
Since she wishes not what she should
But what’s forbidden her finds best.
Now I’m fallen from all grace,
I’ve done well on the asses’ bridge!
And don’t know why I’m in disgrace,
Except I’ve asked a world too much.

Mercy’s lost, and gone from sight
And now I can retrieve it not.
Since she who owns to it of right
Has none to give, and where’s it sought?
How little it seems to those who see –
What would she want with me poor wretch? –
That without her nothing’s here for me,
She lets me die who’ve no help left.

Since with my lady there’s no use
In prayers, her pity, or pleading law,
Nor is she pleased at the news
I love her: then I’ll say no more,
And so depart and swear it’s done!
I’m dead: by death I’ll answer her,
And off I’ll go: she’ll see me gone,
To wretched exile, who knows where?

Tristram, none will hear of me:
Off I’ll go, who knows where?
I’ll sing no more, resigned I’ll be,
And banish joy and love of her.

Listen

Bernart de Ventadorn, “Can vei la lauzeta mover,” sung in Occitan language (as original).
