The Shield of Aeneas

The Shield of Aeneas

Virgil, Aeneid 8.626–731

But Venus, bright among the clouds of heaven,
came bearing gifts; and when, in secret vale,
she saw her son apart by cooling stream,
she stood before him, radiant, and she spoke:

“Behold the promised gifts, my son, fulfilled
by Vulcan’s art. Now do not fear the proud
Laurentines, nor the threats of Turnus’ war.”

She said, and sought her child’s embrace, and laid
the glittering arms beneath an oak before him.
Aeneas gazed, rejoicing in the gifts,
the terrible honor of the god’s own hand.
He could not have enough of them: he turned
his eyes from piece to piece; he wondered, touched,
and lifted in his hands the crested helm,
the sword that breathed out death, the stiff bronze mail,
blood-red and vast, like some dark thundercloud
that burns beneath the sun and flashes fire;
the polished greaves of electrum and gold,
the spear, and then the shield, beyond all words.

There had the lord of fire, who knew the fates,
who knew the age to come, engraved the tale
of Italy, the triumphs of the Romans,
the whole long lineage of Ascanius’ seed,
and all the wars they fought, in order shown.

There too he made the mother wolf, stretched out
within the green cave sacred to Mars’ name;
around her teats the twin boys hung and played,
fearless beneath her, sucking at the beast,
while she bent back her neck and licked each child,
and shaped their bodies gently with her tongue.

Not far from these he set the walls of Rome,
and Sabine women seized from crowded seats
when the great games were held; then sudden war
between the sons of Romulus and old
Tatius, stern king of Cures. Soon the kings,
their strife laid down, stood armed before Jove’s shrine,
holding their cups, and struck a treaty there,
above the slain sow’s body.

Near this, the four-horse chariots tore apart
false Mettus, traitor—Alba, you should have
kept faith. Tullus dragged through the woods the limbs
of him who lied; the bloody brambles dripped.

There Porsenna ordered Rome to take again
her banished Tarquin, and with mighty siege
pressed on the city. Rome’s sons rushed to arms
for freedom. He stood like one enraged, because
Cocles had dared to break the bridge, because
Cloelia, snapping bonds, had crossed the stream.

Upon the height Manlius, guardian of the fort,
stood firm before the temple, holding Rome;
the palace bristled fresh with Romulus’ thatch.
And there, through gilded colonnades, the goose
of silver cried that Gauls were at the gate.
The Gauls moved through the thickets, near the fort,
protected by the darkness and the gift
of shadowy night. Their hair was golden; gold
shone on their cloaks; their striped bright mantles blazed;
their milk-white necks were circled round with gold.
Each brandished two Alpine spears; their long shields
covered their bodies.

Here he had wrought the dancing Salii,
the naked Luperci, wool-bound sacred caps,
and shields that fell from heaven. Chaste mothers rode
in cushioned cars through Rome to solemn rites.

Far off from these, he added Tartarus,
the deep abodes of Dis, the penalties
of crime; and you, Catiline, hung there,
trembling before the Furies’ faces. There
the righteous stood apart, and Cato gave
the dead their laws.

Around these images the sea was poured,
swelling in gold, though hoary foam was white
upon the blue-gray waves; and dolphins bright
with silver swept in circles with their tails
and cleft the flood.

At center: bronze fleets, the Actian war,
Leucate all alive with ordered ships,
and all the waves made golden. Caesar stood
upon the high stern, leading Italy,
the fathers, people, household gods, and great
Penates; from his radiant temples streamed
his father’s star. Elsewhere, with favoring winds
and gods, Agrippa led his towering line;
his brow was bright with naval crown, whose beaks
rose proud, the splendid token of the sea.

Against them, Antony with barbarous wealth
and motley arms, victorious from the East
and crimson shores, brought Egypt, eastern might,
and farthest Bactra; and behind him came—
shame of it—his Egyptian wife.

All rushed together. All the sea was torn,
convulsed by oars, by triple-pointed prows.
They drove for open water. You would think
the Cyclades, uprooted, swam the deep,
or mountains clashed with mountains, such the weight
with which the men pressed on from towered ships.
Flame, tow, and flying steel were hurled by hand;
Neptune’s blue fields grew red with slaughtered men.

The queen amid them called her ships with sistrum,
not yet turning to look at twin snakes near.
Monstrous gods of every shape, and barking
Anubis, raised their weapons against Neptune,
Venus, and Minerva. In the midst,
Mars raged in iron; from the heavens grim
Furies flew; Discord walked with torn robe,
delighted, and Bellona followed her
with bloody whip.

Apollo, watching from Actium’s height,
bent his bow. At that terror, Egypt turned,
and India, Arab, Saba, all took flight.
The queen herself seemed calling winds to aid,
loosening her sails, already pale with death.
The fire-god made her driven by the waves
and western wind through carnage; opposite,
the Nile, in sorrow, opened wide his folds and summoned conquered peoples to his breast,
his sheltering blue.

But Caesar, borne in triple triumph home
through Roman walls, dedicated to the gods
immortal vows: three hundred shrines throughout
the city. Streets rang out with joy and games
and clapping hands. In every temple stood
a choir of mothers; altars in each shrine;
before the altars slaughtered bullocks lay.
Caesar himself, before bright Phoebus’ doors,
sat and reviewed the gifts of conquered lands,
then hung them on the splendid posts. The tribes
of vanquished nations passed in long array,
diverse in dress, in arms, in speech, in mode.
Here Vulcan shaped the Nomads, and the loose-
robed Africans; here Leleges, Carians,
Geloni bearing arrows. Euphrates went
with humbled waves; Morini, farthest men;
the two-horned Rhine; Dahae unsubdued;
and Araxes, indignant at his bridge.

Such scenes he wondered at upon the shield,
his mother’s gift, the fire-god’s unknown tale.
He did not know the things themselves, but joyed
to see their images; and on his shoulder
lifted the fame and fate of sons to come.

The New Colossus

Emma Lazarus

Written in 1883 for the Art Loan Fund Exhibition in Aid of the Bartholdi Pedestal Fund for the Statue of Liberty. Public domain.

Not like the brazen giant of Greek fame,
With conquering limbs astride from land to land;
Here at our sea-washed, sunset gates shall stand
A mighty woman with a torch, whose flame
Is the imprisoned lightning, and her name
Mother of Exiles. From her beacon-hand
Glows world-wide welcome; her mild eyes command
The air-bridged harbor that twin cities frame.

“Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!” cries she
With silent lips. “Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!”

The Old Colossus and the New

Lazarus constructs her national ideal through a deliberate contrast between two monumental figures. The ancient Colossus appears as an emblem of conquest, bestriding a harbour with weapons and flame. The “new colossus” preserves the immense scale and the torch but transforms their meaning: power becomes protection, and the harbour becomes an entrance for exiles rather than a boundary defended against them.

Essential Questions

  • Recalling previous unit’s passage — Homer describing the forging of Achilles’ shield — what are the similarities and differences we can identify in this passage?
  • Is Virgil doing something different with the picture he paints? What is his purpose?
  • Is this passage myth-making, is it prophecy, is it propaganda?
  • What is the significance of Aeneas carrying this particular object into battle with him?
Advanced Questions Show Hide
  • Which shield — Achilles’ or Aeneas’, Homer’s or Virgil’s — depicts a whole World, and which depicts the particular?
  • Is one more idealogical than the other?
  • How does Virgil’s passage regard violence and war as compared to Homer’s?
  • Why might Virgil desire to absorb this particular bit of what is essentially Ancient Greece’s origin mythology into that of Rome?
  • How does this passage regard rites and sacrifices?
  • What can we say about Aeneas not recognizing or understanding what he sees depicted on the shield?

Vocabulary

  • Epic — a long narrative poem concerned with heroic action, divine forces, and communal destiny
  • Teleology — the interpretation of events as moving toward a predetermined end or purpose
  • Propaganda — communication designed to shape public belief or feeling in the service of a political, religious, or ideological cause. It does not have to be false; it often works by selecting, arranging, beautifying, simplifying, or emotionally charging facts so that the audience sees one interpretation as natural, noble, or necessary
  • Dactylic hexameter — the classical epic meter used by Homer and Virgil. Each line has six metrical feet, built mainly from dactyls — long-short-short — and spondees — long-long. In Latin, the meter is based on syllable length, not stress accent.
Teacher notes Show Hide
  • Notice that the images of the shield occupy time as well as space
  • Notice that the history told is not merely triumphal. The whole sweep of Roman formation is there, with all its violence and all its burden.
  • Think of the intended reader of this ekphrasis, a Roman knowing and recognizing the future which Aeneas carries with him but does not yet know himself.
  • Recall Homer’s emphasis on personal and social life. Rites, dancing, rituals, games. Here the images describe are national.
